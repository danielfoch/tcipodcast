Canadian deals, Micron’s AI boom and Nike’s turnaround

Simon Belanger and Daniel Kent discuss Canadian M&A, Cobre Panama, Micron’s cycle, Carnival’s buybacks and the consumer signals in Nike and AutoZone.

By TCI Podcast Network · 2026-10-08
Based on the conversation with Simon Belanger and Daniel Kent

Episode details & show notes

Canadian dealmaking: focus on the business fitDescript transcript: 00:02:26–00:05:48 · 00:07:02–00:09:36

Canadian mergers and acquisitions open the episode. Simon Belanger and Daniel Kent discuss the proposed Emera–Canadian Utilities combination and Cenovus’s move to buy Athabasca Oil. The conversation centres on what the combined businesses could look like: geographic exposure, neighbouring assets and the practical opportunities created by greater scale.

For the utilities, the hosts look at how the transaction would change the mix of Canadian and international operations. In the oil sands, Daniel highlights the proximity of Athabasca’s assets to operations Cenovus already owns. He sees that location as an important part of the acquisition’s rationale, while acknowledging that he is not making a definitive judgement on the offer’s value.

Cobre Panama: a recommendation is not a final decisionDescript transcript: 00:13:48–00:18:19

Simon revisits the previous episode’s discussion of Cobre Panama after reading more about the commission’s recommendations. His central clarification is that a long, staged closure with continued production would be very different from an immediate shutdown. That distinction changes how he interprets the news for First Quantum and, through its streaming interest, Franco-Nevada.

The uncertainty remains important. Simon explicitly notes that the government has not made its final decision. This part of the discussion is an interpretation of the proposal and possible outcomes, rather than confirmation that the mine will reopen. The hosts also discuss the economic and rehabilitation costs that make the decision more complicated than a simple open-or-close headline.

Micron: strong earnings do not remove the cycleDescript transcript: 00:22:07–00:24:34 · 00:25:03–00:27:23

Micron’s results prompt a broader discussion of the memory-chip industry. Daniel connects the company’s strength to pricing and tight supply, then turns to the time required to add manufacturing capacity. New factories take years to arrive; the question is whether demand will still justify that capacity when it becomes available.

That leads to the episode’s valuation lesson. A low price-to-earnings ratio can look reassuring when profits are unusually strong, but the denominator may be near a cyclical peak. Daniel remains cautious despite the operating momentum. The hosts consider whether longer-term take-or-pay contracts offer more protection this time, without treating those contracts as a guarantee against a future downturn.

Carnival: buybacks or a stronger balance sheet?Descript transcript: 00:33:56–00:36:28

The Carnival discussion becomes a debate about capital allocation. Simon questions the decision to buy back shares while the company still has debt to manage. His preference is for greater financial flexibility, particularly in a business that experienced severe disruption during the pandemic.

The conversation also includes a counterweight to that criticism: Simon notes an improvement in the company’s debt rating. The article’s point is therefore the trade-off the hosts raise, not a claim that every buyback is a mistake. Management must weigh the perceived value of repurchasing stock against the resilience that debt reduction can provide.

Nike and AutoZone: two views of a pressured consumerDescript transcript: 00:37:22–00:44:26 · 00:45:27–00:46:56

Daniel walks through Nike’s continued difficulties, including weaker demand in China and the challenges of its distribution reset. Simon asks whether the brand can rebuild its premium position while customers are looking harder for value. He also distinguishes value from simply choosing the cheapest product: buyers still need to feel that what they receive justifies what they pay.

AutoZone provides a different angle. Simon contrasts softer do-it-yourself demand with stronger commercial sales and discusses customers postponing repairs or trading down. Taken together, these sections show why the hosts look beyond a headline sales figure. The customer, the channel and the reason for spending—or waiting—can tell different stories within the same business.
