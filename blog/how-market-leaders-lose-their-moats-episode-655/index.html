How market leaders lose their moats: four lessons

Simon Belanger and Daniel Kent examine AOL, EpiPen, BlackBerry and Nike—and the management decisions that weakened their competitive advantages.

By TCI Podcast Network · 2026-10-05
Based on the conversation with Simon Belanger and Daniel Kent

Listen to the full episode

Episode details & show notes

AOL: dependency can look like loyaltyDescript transcript: 00:10:47–00:15:38

Why do customers stay with a business? In episode 655, Simon Belanger and Daniel Kent use four former market leaders to examine how management decisions can weaken a competitive advantage. Their discussion begins with AOL, where Simon distinguishes customer loyalty from dependence on a service that once provided access to the internet.

In Simon’s account, broadband changed that relationship. Customers could reach the web without AOL, while email, search and other parts of its bundle became available elsewhere. He argues that management did not adapt quickly enough to this shift. The lesson he draws is to understand why customers pay today, and whether that reason will survive a change in technology.

EpiPen: pricing power has limitsDescript transcript: 00:23:34–00:27:54

Daniel uses Mylan’s EpiPen business to discuss a different risk: pushing pricing power too far. His criticism centres on management’s use of a strong market position to raise prices, and the public and political backlash he describes when those costs became more visible to customers.

The hosts frame this as a warning about the durability of an advantage. Being able to charge more does not necessarily mean doing so strengthens the business over time. Daniel’s interpretation is that a less aggressive approach could have preserved a longer runway. His argument is about management’s decisions, rather than a forecast for the business today.

BlackBerry: strong results can hide a changing marketDescript transcript: 00:34:10–00:44:57

Simon’s BlackBerry example highlights the danger of relying on recent financial performance as proof that a competitive position is secure. He describes a company whose shipments and revenues continued to grow even as customers became interested in touchscreens, richer browsing and app ecosystems.

In the hosts’ discussion, the challenge expanded beyond competing handsets. Developers and users increasingly reinforced the appeal of Apple and Android, while BlackBerry remained attached to assumptions about keyboards and enterprise customers. Simon’s broader observation is that customer preferences can shift underneath healthy reported results. Waiting for the financial statements to show the damage may leave management with less time to respond.

Nike: distribution was part of the advantageDescript transcript: 00:45:05–00:58:13

Daniel argues that Nike’s retail presence was an important part of its moat. Reducing wholesale distribution in favour of direct sales might appear attractive on margins, but he points out that selling directly also brings responsibilities such as stores, staffing, shipping and returns.

He believes the pandemic made the strategy look stronger than it was, while reduced shelf presence gave competing brands room to reach shoppers. Simon adds the practical example of customers wanting to try shoes in person. The discussion ends with caution about assuming a turnaround: Simon says he would prefer to see evidence of improvement before acting. Across these examples, the hosts keep returning to the same question—what actually makes customers choose the business?
